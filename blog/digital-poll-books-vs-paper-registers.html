Digital Poll Books vs Paper Registers: What Changes for Polling Station Teams?

Polling station technology13 November 2026T2K Group6 min read

At a busy polling station, finding an elector on the register is only one part of the job. Staff must complete the required checks, issue the correct ballot papers and maintain accurate records, while giving electors the help they need. Every administrative step has to work when several people arrive at once.

Digital poll books can make parts of that work easier by replacing page searches and some manual recording with electronic processes. For teams considering Digital Poll Clerk, the practical question is how those changes will affect the people running the station throughout polling day.

A digital poll book is an electronic polling station register, typically accessed on a tablet or laptop, that staff use to find electors and mark them as having voted. The Electoral Commission describes this use of electronic registers in its UK flexible voting feasibility studies. [1]

Functions vary between systems. Any digital workflow needs to fit the procedures approved for the particular election. [2, 3, 4]

Finding the right elector

Electronic search gives staff another way to locate an elector without working through a printed register. This can be particularly useful when the station needs access to a large set of records. The Electoral Commission identifies searchable registers as one of the practical advantages of electronic poll books. [3]

Staff still need to confirm that they have selected the right entry and follow the applicable eligibility and identity checks. During training, practise searches involving common surnames and similar addresses. Measure the whole check-in process during a trial, including the time needed to resolve queries, to understand what a change could mean for queues.

Reducing repeated recording

In the paper process described in the Commission’s guidance for England’s May 2026 local elections, staff mark the register and write the elector number against the ballot paper number on the corresponding number list. Those are separate recording tasks during the same interaction. [2]

Some digital systems automate associated records. In the Commission’s evaluation of the 2026 flexible voting pilots, the systems recorded corresponding number list entries without staff having to write them manually. Staff could complete ballot issue without making that additional handwritten entry. [4]

Automation still needs checks. Before rollout, test how the proposed system handles corrections, spoilt ballot papers and combined polls where relevant. Staff should understand what a completed action looks like and how to raise a discrepancy. At close of poll, the ballot paper account and the handling of physical materials remain essential. [2]

Preparing and training the team

Paper registers require preparation too. The Commission’s register guidance calls for checks that records are complete, reflect changes and belong to the correct station. Digital deployment adds equipment and configuration checks to that work. [8]

A useful pre-election rehearsal should confirm the correct election data, staff access, power arrangements and any connections between devices or other systems. The Electoral Commission’s contingency guidance for local elections in England recommends working with IT services on support, locally accessible files and spare equipment in case of IT failure. Include those arrangements in the rehearsal. [6]

Give staff hands-on practice with routine check-in, relevant proxy and postal-elector scenarios, corrections and the fallback procedure. Make the support route clear before polling day, including which questions go to the Presiding Officer and which need technical help.

Keep elector care central to that training. The Electoral Commission’s guidance covers communication, accessibility, personal data and close-of-poll duties alongside the polling process. A device changes the administrative tool; staff still need the knowledge and confidence to assist electors. [5]

Seeing activity during the day

Connected registers can give election managers a more current picture of polling activity. The 2026 pilots included live turnout dashboards and information that helped electoral services teams direct support towards busier stations. [4]

Ask what each figure represents and how recently it was updated. A count of electors checked in or ballot papers issued is different from the count of votes cast for candidates. Teams should also know how the system indicates a lost connection or an update awaiting synchronisation.

Some systems can continue operating without a connection, but that does not guarantee access to current information from other devices or locations. Where a voting model depends on shared status, the permitted response to a connection failure needs to be agreed in advance. [3]

Planning for interruptions and protecting data

Paper registers do not depend on a charged device or a working network. Digital deployment therefore needs a clear plan for equipment and connectivity failures. The Electoral Commission’s flexible voting feasibility studies recommend paper electoral registers as a backup at flexible voting locations in case of power or connectivity problems. [3]

Make the fallback usable at the desk. Staff need to know who authorises the change, which records to use and how to document ballot issue while the system is unavailable. The plan should also explain how records will be reconciled before normal operation resumes, so staff avoid duplicate or missing entries.

Protecting elector information also becomes part of device handling. The Electoral Commission’s data protection guidance calls for safeguards in election plans and risk registers, and for staff training to reflect data protection requirements. It identifies lost or stolen devices containing register data as a potential personal data breach. Make sure staff know how to report a lost device or suspected breach. [7]

What recent UK pilots show

The Electoral Commission’s evaluation of the 2026 flexible voting pilots reported reduced polling-station workload and generally positive experiences with digital registers. It also found substantial preparation and support demands. In Milton Keynes, some staff used paper registers during connectivity problems, returning to tablets when the connection recovered. [4]

The evaluation involved four volunteer local authorities, so its findings cannot confidently predict results elsewhere. [4] For a team planning adoption, that makes a local rehearsal valuable: it can reveal whether the proposed workflow suits the venues, staff and support arrangements actually available.

Taking the next step with Digital Poll Clerk

Start by identifying the tasks your team wants to improve. Use those tasks to structure a walkthrough, then assess search, recording, training, reporting and recovery against your own polling-day requirements. Include preparation time and support costs when judging the overall value.

Confirm the requirements for the election being administered. For example, the Commission’s Senedd guidance explicitly allows the register to be supplied in paper or electronic form. That is election-specific guidance; a rollout should follow the applicable rules and the Returning Officer’s procedures. [8]

A successful change should leave polling station teams confident about finding the right record, completing the required work and handling exceptions. Those are practical outcomes to assess when considering Digital Poll Clerk.
